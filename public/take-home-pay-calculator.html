Gross Pay vs. Net Pay: What Is Take-Home Pay?

Gross pay is what you earn before anything is withheld. It is the number on a job offer, a mortgage application and most salary comparisons. Net pay, also called take-home pay or disposable income, is the amount deposited after taxes and payroll deductions. Budgeting from net pay is more realistic, because rent, groceries and savings are paid with the money you actually receive.

Enter your gross annual salary in the first field. The final paycheck figure shows your net pay per pay period.

Example: Take-Home Pay on an $80,000 Salary

A single filer earning $80,000 with $6,000 in pre-tax deductions and no state tax has about $57,900 of federal taxable income after the 2026 standard deduction. That produces roughly $7,450 in federal income tax, plus about $4,960 in Social Security and $1,160 in Medicare. The remaining yearly amount is near $60,400, or roughly $2,320 per bi-weekly paycheck. This is an illustration only; use the calculator above for your own numbers.

Pay Frequency Explained

Pay FrequencyDescription
DailyPaid each working day. Uncommon for salaried jobs.
Weekly52 paychecks a year, usually on the same weekday.
Bi-weekly26 paychecks a year, one every other week.
Semi-monthly24 paychecks a year, on set dates such as the 15th and the last day of the month.
Monthly12 paychecks a year, on one set day each month.
Quarterly4 paychecks a year. Uncommon.
Semi-annually2 paychecks a year. Uncommon.
Annually1 payment a year. Uncommon.

Bi-weekly vs. semi-monthly: bi-weekly pay produces 26 checks, so each one is smaller than a semi-monthly check (24 per year) even though the annual total is identical. Twice a year, bi-weekly employees receive a third paycheck in a single month. Pay frequency does not change how much tax you owe for the year. Federal law only requires that your pay schedule be regular and predictable, while some states set their own minimum pay frequencies.

Deductions That Reduce Your Taxable Income

1. Pre-tax deductions withheld by your employer

Taken from your pay before income tax is applied: traditional 401(k) contributions, your share of health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, child support payments, and union or uniform dues.

2. Deductions not withheld

Claimed on your tax return rather than through payroll: IRA contributions, student loan interest and qualified education expenses of up to $4,000.

3. Itemized deductions or the standard deduction

Itemized deductions include mortgage interest, charitable gifts, medical costs above 10% of adjusted gross income, and state and local taxes up to the legal limit. Most people take the standard deduction instead: $16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for joint filers and $24,150 for heads of household in 2026. Choose whichever is larger.

New deductions for 2025–2028

The One Big Beautiful Bill added temporary deductions that you can claim whether you itemize or not. They are scheduled to expire after 2028 unless Congress extends them.

  • Tips: up to $25,000 per year; phases out above $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income ($300,000 joint).
  • Overtime: up to $12,500 per year ($25,000 joint); same phase-out thresholds as tips.
  • Car loan interest: up to $10,000 per year on a qualified vehicle; phases out above $100,000 ($200,000 joint).
  • Seniors (65+): an extra $6,000 per person; phases out above $75,000 ($150,000 joint).

Taxes Withheld From Your Paycheck

Employers must withhold income and payroll taxes. Self-employed workers and independent contractors pay these themselves through quarterly or annual payments. To estimate a full tax return instead, use our Income Tax Calculator.

Federal Income Tax

Federal income tax is progressive: each slice of taxable income is taxed at its own rate, from 10% up to a top rate of 37%. Only income above the bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate. It is typically the largest deduction on a paycheck and is collected by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS).

State Income Tax

Seven states have no wage income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, South Dakota, Texas, Washington and Wyoming. New Hampshire and Tennessee do not tax wages but have historically taxed some investment income. Eight states use a flat rate: Colorado, Illinois, Indiana, Massachusetts, Michigan, North Carolina, Pennsylvania and Utah. Others use progressive brackets, and California has the highest top rate at 13.30%. Enter your effective rate in the state field above.

City and Local Income Tax

Most U.S. cities and counties do not levy an income tax, but some do, affecting roughly 10% of Americans. The highest local rates are concentrated in large cities such as New York City.

FICA: Social Security and Medicare

FICA is the payroll tax. Employees and employers each pay half; self-employed people pay both halves. Social Security is 6.2% of wages (12.4% combined) up to $184,500 in 2026 ($176,100 in 2025). See the Social Security Calculator for benefit estimates. Medicare is 1.45% of all wages (2.9% combined). Employees owe an additional 0.9% on wages above these thresholds:

How to Increase Your Take-Home Pay

  • Ask for a raise or change jobs. Use the Salary Calculator to compare offers. Switching employers is often where the largest pay jumps happen.
  • Review payroll deductions. Compare health, dental, life and disability options, and check whether a spouse’s plan covers the family more cheaply.
  • Use an FSA or HSA. Contributions come out before income tax, which lowers your tax bill for spending you were going to do anyway.
  • Work overtime. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, non-exempt employees earn at least 1.5 times their regular rate beyond 40 hours a week. Track hours with the Time Card Calculator.
  • Cash out unused PTO if your employer allows it.
  • Pause 401(k) contributions only in a real cash crunch. If you pause, try to keep contributing enough to earn your full employer match.

Once you know your net pay, plug it into the Budget Calculator (which asks for pre-tax income, so enter your gross figure there).

Take-Home Pay FAQ

How do I calculate my take-home pay?

Subtract pre-tax deductions from gross pay, then subtract federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and any state or city tax. Divide what remains by your number of pay periods.

Why is my paycheck smaller than my salary divided by pay periods?

Taxes and benefit costs are withheld before you are paid. Salary is gross; your deposit is net.

What is the 2026 standard deduction?

$16,100 for single filers, $32,200 for married filing jointly and $24,150 for head of household.

What is the difference between bi-weekly and semi-monthly pay?

Bi-weekly is 26 paychecks a year; semi-monthly is 24. Annual pay is the same, but bi-weekly checks are smaller.

Does the additional Medicare tax apply to me?

Only if your wages exceed $200,000 (single), $250,000 (married filing jointly) or $125,000 (married filing separately). The extra rate is 0.9%.
