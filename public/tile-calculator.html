How to Use the Tile Calculator

Enter the length and width of one tile, then enter the area to cover either as length and width (Use Dimensions) or as a single figure (Use Total Area). Enter the gap between tiles, such as the grout line, or a negative value if the tiles overlap. Optionally enter the number of tiles per box and a price, then click Calculate. The result is an estimate, so add extra tiles for cuts and breakage before you buy.

Example 1: A floor with grout gaps

A 20 ft by 15 ft floor is covered with 12 in by 12 in tiles and a 0.25 in gap.

area = 20 × 15 = 300 sq ft = 43,200 sq in
one tile with gap = 12.25 × 12.25 = 150.06 sq in
tiles = 43,200 ÷ 150.06 ≈ 288

Example 2: Adding waste and finding boxes

Using the 288 tiles from Example 1, order 10% extra and buy boxes of 12 tiles.

tiles with waste = 288 × 1.10 = 316.8, rounded up to 317
boxes = 317 ÷ 12 = 26.4, rounded up to 27 boxes

Example 3: Estimating cost

If the same 300 sq ft floor uses tile priced at $3 per square foot:

cost = 300 × $3 = $900

Gap Size

Floor or wall tiles are typically installed with gaps between the tiles because the average tile may look quite similar to the next tile, but they are often not uniform in shape and size, and would not fit properly if installed without any gaps. These gaps are most commonly filled with grout, and as such, are often referred to as grout size, or grout lines. Grout is a form of concrete, and the gap between tiles can range from anywhere between one-sixteenth of an inch to half an inch in size. Different sized tiles, materials, and design needs all affect the size of the gaps. Although it is typically more difficult to have small gaps between tiles due to lack of a uniform shape and size, the use of rectified tiles (tiles that undergo additional processing to ensure that they are uniform) can allow for smaller spacing, though at an additional cost. For more uniformly cut tiles such as granite, smaller grout spacing can result in less visibility of grout lines between each tile.

In some cases, such as with roofing tile or the wood siding of a wall, rather than having a gap between them, tiles overlap to prevent leakage. The tile calculator can account for both of these situations. Either enter a positive value if there is a gap between the tiles being used, or a negative value if the tiles overlap.

Tile Installation Options

Size

Tile size can range anywhere from smaller mosaics that are 3/8", to 24" × 48" slab tiles and everything in between. Square sizes (same width and length) are the most popular, accessible, and easiest to install. While straight edge tiles (rectangular, square, parallelogram) are the most common, unique tile shapes also exist, though installation is not as easy. Large tile sizes can make smaller rooms appear bigger, as well as more open and clean because there are fewer grout lines. However, installing larger tiles results in more wastage, while using smaller tiles can help add texture to a room.

Material

There are a number of different classifications of tiles, including ceramic, porcelain, glass, quarry, and stone. Ceramic and porcelain tiles are the most cost efficient, and come in a variety of different styles. Glass tiles, while not appropriate for flooring because they crack under pressure, are visually unique and interesting; they are most commonly used for kitchen and bathroom backsplashes. Quarry tiles have rough surfaces that are good for floors that require grip, and are commonly used outdoors and in restaurant kitchens. Stone tiles include marble and granite, which provide unique and natural stone patterns, textures, and colors that are difficult to achieve using ceramics. They also offer the illusion of blending into grout edges, giving off an overall uniform look.

Layout Pattern

There are many different patterns used when installing tiles. The most common pattern used is a linear grid, with square or rectangular tiles, or a pattern involving angled squares or rectangles that form a typical diamond shape.

Running bond layouts (like those used with brick walls) involve offset rows or columns of tiles, usually with a 2:1 length to edge ratio. Running bonds generally use about 10% more material.

Herringbones involve aligning tiles in angled patterns, usually 45 or 90 degrees. Similar to running bond layouts, herringbone layouts use more material than linear layouts.

Frequently Asked Questions

Click a question to see the answer.

How do I calculate how many tiles I need?

Divide the area to cover by the area of one tile, including the gap. The formula is area ÷ ((tile length + gap) × (tile width + gap)). For example, a 20 ft by 15 ft floor is 300 square feet, and 12 inch tiles with a 0.25 inch gap need about 288 tiles.


How much extra tile should I order for waste?

A common rule of thumb is to order about 10% extra to cover cuts, breakage and future repairs. Diagonal, running bond and herringbone layouts, and rooms with many corners, can need more. For example, 288 tiles plus 10% is 317 tiles.


What gap or grout size should I enter?

Grout gaps typically range from 1/16 inch to 1/2 inch, depending on the tile size, material and design. Rectified tiles, which are cut more uniformly, allow smaller gaps. Enter the gap you plan to use, or 0 if there is none.


How do I account for overlapping tiles, such as roof tiles?

Enter a negative gap value. A positive value means there is a gap between tiles, such as grout, and a negative value means the tiles overlap, as with roofing tile or wood siding.


How do I calculate how many boxes of tile to buy?

Divide the number of tiles you need by the number of tiles per box and round up to a whole box. For example, 317 tiles at 12 tiles per box is 26.4, so buy 27 boxes.


Can I enter the total area instead of the length and width?

Yes. Choose Use Total Area and enter the area in square feet, square meters, square yards, square inches or square centimeters. If you add several rooms, add their areas together first.


Does the tile layout pattern change how many tiles I need?

Yes. A straight linear grid wastes the least. Running bond layouts generally use about 10% more material, and herringbone layouts also use more than a linear layout because more tiles must be cut.


How do I estimate the cost of tile?

Multiply the number of tiles, boxes or square feet by the price per unit. For example, 300 square feet at $3 per square foot is $900. Enter a price in the optional Price field to include cost in your estimate.
