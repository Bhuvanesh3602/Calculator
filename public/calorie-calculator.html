How the Calorie Calculator Works: BMR Formulas

The results are an estimated average based on three equations. The Harris-Benedict equation was one of the earliest ways to calculate basal metabolic rate (BMR), the energy your body uses at rest. It was revised in 1984 and used until the Mifflin-St Jeor equation arrived in 1990, which has been shown to be more accurate. Katch-McArdle instead calculates resting daily energy expenditure using lean body mass, so it can be more accurate for lean people who know their body fat percentage.

Mifflin-St Jeor Equation

Men: BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5
Women: BMR = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161

Revised Harris-Benedict Equation

Men: BMR = 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A + 88.362
Women: BMR = 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A + 447.593

Katch-McArdle Formula

BMR = 370 + 21.6(1 − F)W

Where W is body weight in kg, H is height in cm, A is age in years, and F is body fat as a decimal (for example 0.20 for 20%).

Your BMR is multiplied by an activity factor (generally 1.2–1.95) to estimate the calories needed to maintain your weight. Because 1 pound (about 0.45 kg) is roughly 3,500 calories, eating 500 calories per day below maintenance should lose about 1 pound per week. For example, with a 2,500 calorie maintenance level, eating 2,000 calories daily for a week is a 3,500 calorie deficit.

Losing more than 2 pounds per week is not recommended: it likely involves muscle loss, which lowers BMR, and can include dehydration. Keep a balanced diet with enough fiber and nutrients, especially when exercising, and see a doctor or registered dietitian before attempting faster loss.

Calorie Counting as a Means for Weight Loss

Calorie counting for weight loss can be broken into a few steps:

  1. Find your BMR with one of the equations above. These are approximations, so cutting exactly 500 calories will not always give exactly 1 pound lost per week.
  2. Set your goal. A reduction of about 500 calories per day targets 1 pound per week; the maximum is generally about 1,000 per day (2 pounds per week). Consult your doctor or a registered dietitian nutritionist (RDN) if you plan to lose more than 2 pounds per week.
  3. Choose a tracking method: a smartphone app, website, spreadsheet or pen-and-paper journal. Measuring your typical meals for a while makes it much easier to estimate portions later.
  4. Track progress over time. Weight alone does not determine health, and daily weight swings come from water and timing. Compare weekly averages and weigh yourself under consistent conditions, such as after waking and before breakfast.
  5. Keep at it!

Calorie counting is not an exact science and does not account for macronutrients. There is no known ideal split of fats, proteins and carbohydrates, but minimally processed plant and animal foods tend to support healthy weight loss and maintenance. In its simplest form, calories consumed minus calories expended gives weight gain when positive and weight loss when negative, but many other factors affect sustainable weight loss.

Foods that need more chewing and digestion cause the body to burn slightly more calories (the thermic effect of food), and vegetables tend to provide more nutrients for fewer calories than processed foods. A person on the "Twinkie diet" lost 27 pounds in two months by counting calories alone, but that approach is not recommended because of unknown long-term effects on conditions such as cancer, heart disease and diabetes.

Counting calories also raises nutritional awareness. Many people underestimate their intake, and learning what is in a bag of chips and how little it satisfies hunger makes portion control easier. Concrete calorie goals are easier to follow than "eat less", and eating from a smaller plate can reduce intake. Seeing how much exercise it takes to burn off a 220-calorie bag of M&M's can also push people toward healthier choices.

In the end, choose a strategy you can stick to. Calorie counting is one method among many, and the best approach is the one that fits your lifestyle.

Zigzag Calorie Cycling

Zigzag calorie cycling aims to stop the body adapting to a lower calorie intake, which can cause weight loss plateaus. You alternate high-calorie and low-calorie days while hitting the same weekly total.

For example, with a target of 14,000 calories per week you could eat 2,300 calories on three days and 1,775 on the other four, or 2,000 every day. Either way the weekly total is 14,000. The flexibility can help you plan around social events or a "cheat day".

There is no concrete rule on how to alternate. High and low days commonly differ by about 200-300 calories, with the high day often equal to your maintenance level; people with higher activity levels can use a larger difference. The calculator shows two zigzag schedules: one with two higher and five lower days, and one that increases and decreases gradually.

How Many Calories Do You Need?

Your needs depend on age, weight, height, sex, activity level and overall health. A physically active 25-year-old man who is 6 feet tall needs far more than a sedentary 70-year-old woman who is 5 feet tall. Adult men generally need 2,000-3,000 calories per day to maintain weight and adult women 1,600-2,400, according to the U.S. Department of Health.

Eating too few calories makes the body function poorly. Harvard Health Publications suggests at least 1,200 calories for women and 1,500 for men unless supervised by a doctor.

Calories: Different Kinds and Their Effects

Most calories come from carbohydrates, protein and fat, and for many people also alcohol, which adds many empty calories. Label calories and the calories your body actually retains can differ, which is one reason opinions on the "best" way to lose weight conflict. Chewing more is linked to eating less and burning slightly more calories, though these effects are not fully understood.

Foods that take effort to chew, such as fruit, vegetables, lean meats and whole grains, use more energy to digest and keep you full longer. Coffee, tea, chilies, cinnamon and ginger may slightly raise calories burned.

Foods can be high-calorie (calorie-dense), low-calorie, or "empty calories" (added sugars and solid fats with few nutrients). High-calorie does not mean unhealthy: avocados, quinoa, nuts and whole grains are calorie-dense but healthful in moderation. Studies suggest 500 calories of carrots and 500 calories of popcorn are not handled identically by the body. Drinks make up an estimated 21% of a typical person's calories, and many are empty calories. Water, tea and coffee without sugar are the best choices.

Remember: all foods should be eaten in moderation. Natural foods like fruit can contain a lot of sugar, and "health foods" such as reduced-fat products often add sugar to make up for lost taste, so check what is actually in a product.

Calorie Calculator FAQ

How many calories do I need per day?

It depends on your age, sex, height, weight and activity level. Adult men generally need about 2,000–3,000 calories per day to maintain weight and adult women about 1,600–2,400, according to US government dietary guidance. Use the calculator above for a personal estimate.

How do I calculate my daily calories?

First estimate your BMR with the Mifflin-St Jeor equation (men: 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5; women: 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161, with W in kg, H in cm, A in years). Then multiply by an activity factor, from 1.2 for sedentary to 1.9 for extra active.

How many calories should I cut to lose 1 pound a week?

One pound of body weight is roughly 3,500 calories, so a deficit of about 500 calories per day below your maintenance level is expected to lose about 1 pound per week. Real results vary from person to person.

What is the safest rate of weight loss?

Losing about 1–2 pounds (0.5–1 kg) per week is generally considered safe. Losing faster can mean muscle loss and may lower metabolism. Talk to a doctor or registered dietitian before attempting a larger deficit.

What is the minimum number of calories per day?

Harvard Health Publications suggests at least 1,200 calories for women and 1,500 for men unless supervised by a doctor.

Which BMR formula is most accurate?

Mifflin-St Jeor is generally considered the most accurate for most people. Katch-McArdle can be more accurate for lean people who know their body fat percentage, because it accounts for lean body mass.

What is the difference between a calorie and a kilojoule?

Both measure food energy. 1 nutritional Calorie (kcal) equals 4.1868 kilojoules (kJ). Use the Food Energy Converter on this page to convert between units.

What is zigzag calorie cycling?

Zigzag calorie cycling alternates higher and lower calorie days while keeping the same weekly total, to add flexibility and help avoid plateaus.
